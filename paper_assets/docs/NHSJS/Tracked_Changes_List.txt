Delete             We propose a two-stage sequential filtering framework in which news articles and financial reports are evaluated separately. In Stage One the model analyzes historical news to identify candidate opportunities; in Stage Two financial reports are used to validate and refine those candidates into a final portfolio. Delete             The evaluation enforces strict temporal constraints throughout to eliminate the look-ahead and data-snooping biases identified by Li et al. (2025) and others. Insert             The S&P 500 comparison overstates selection skill, because the eligible universe is concentrated in technology and semiconductor names that outran the broad market over the study period. Against progressively harder benchmarks, GPT-5.1’s pooled beat rate falls from 96.5% (S&P 500) to 94.3% (S&P 500 Information Technology, XLK), then to 39.6% against an equal-weighted portfolio of the eligible universe itself and 32.2% against a pure-semiconductor index (SMH), the hardest comparison because semiconductors led the sector over the study period. The filtering effect, however, survives these harder comparisons: 20→10 outperforms the equal-weighted universe in 81.4% of runs and SMH in 68.3%, and 10→5 in 64.8% and 60.0% respectively, while the unfiltered baseline does so in only 18.5% and 6.7%. All comparisons in this subsection are pooled across the full run set.
Insert             The eligible comparison set is a fixed list of 29 large-capitalization technology and semiconductor companies (Supplementary Information), used only for the unfiltered baseline and as the draw pool for the equal-weighted and random benchmarks; the filtered configurations never see it, nominating candidates freely from screened news. Every constituent was continuously listed across the full evaluation span, so none was acquired or delisted and the benchmark needs no point-in-time reconstruction. The residual exposure is omission: companies large at the earliest window starts but no longer qualifying would not appear. The set does retain constituents whose prices declined materially, so it is not a selection of winners only, but this bounds the benchmark rather than the pipeline.
Insert             Random portfolios drawn from the eligible universe, matched to each portfolio’s holding count and equal-weighted, provide a direct test of selection skill: relative to 4,000 such draws per run, the median GPT-5.1 portfolio under 20→10 sits at the 75th percentile and under 10→5 at the 69th, whereas the unfiltered baseline sits at the 40th percentile and the aggressive 30-pool configurations near the 17th–21st. Moderate two-stage filtering is thus the difference between below-random and above-random selection within the sector.
Insert             Two ablations probe the robustness of the effect. Removing the single largest return contributor from every portfolio lowers GPT-5.1’s 20→10 mean ROI from 60.6% to 52.5% and the unfiltered baseline from 45.7% to 39.4%, so the matched filtering edge narrows only modestly, from about +14.9 to +13.1 percentage points; excluding all semiconductor holdings (with weights renormalized) leaves a smaller edge of +3.0 points. The filtering advantage therefore does not rest on any single position—it survives removing each portfolio’s best contributor largely intact—but it operates substantially through semiconductor selection: once the semiconductor sector as a whole is excluded, the matched edge, while still positive, is modest.
Insert             Finally, re-scoring every portfolio at equal weights isolates stock selection from allocation weighting: GPT-5.1’s model-assigned weights cost 4.6 percentage points of mean ROI relative to equal-weighting the same picks (Wilcoxon p < 0.0001; GPT-4o −1.2, GPT-4o-mini ≈ 0). The models’ selection adds value; their position sizing subtracts from it. Sector-index returns, random-portfolio percentiles, ablation tables, and per-configuration risk metrics (annualized volatility, maximum drawdown, Sharpe, Sortino) are provided in the Supplementary Information.
Insert             Risk-adjusted performance moves in the same direction as raw return. For GPT-5.1, the best configuration (20→10) earns a median Sharpe ratio of 1.70 and Sortino of 2.66, against 1.34 and 2.04 for the unfiltered baseline and 0.98 for the S&P 500 over the same windows; the weakest configuration (30→5) falls to 1.30. Annualized volatility is similar across configurations (about 22–28%) and maximum drawdowns cluster between −22% and −24%, so the return gains from moderate filtering are not obtained by taking proportionally more risk. Full volatility, drawdown, and risk-adjusted statistics for every configuration and model are reported in the Supplementary Information.
