p5   ParagraphProperty  Scope and Limitations
p5   Insert             Throughout the paper, “long-term” denotes these 12-month buy-and-hold horizons: long relative to the intraday-to-quarterly horizons that dominate the prior LLM-trading literature, though medium-term by traditional investing standards. The model is not restricted to a predefined ticker list: in every filtered configuration it proposes candidate companies freely from the screened news, so the effective universe is open and emerges from contemporaneous coverage rather than a curated survivor list. A fixed set of large-capitalization technology and semiconductor names is used only for the unfiltered single-pass baseline and as the eligible comparison set for the benchmark analyses.
p6   Delete             Methodology Overview We propose a two-stage sequential filtering framework in which news articles and financial reports are evaluated separately. In Stage One the model analyzes historical news to identify candidate opportunities; in Stage Two financial reports are used to validate and refine those candidates into a final portfolio. This decomposition constrains the model to a well-defined reasoning task at each stage, letting us directly evaluate the impact of structural organization of information on investment performance. The evaluation enforces strict temporal constraints throughout to eliminate the look-ahead and data-snooping biases identified by Li et al. (2025) and others. All input data are sourced from dates strictly preceding each evaluation window, and model-specific training-cutoff restrictions further reduce lookahead risk, ensuring reported performance reflects genuine analytical capability rather than latent knowledge from pre-training.

p7   Insert             Because a fiscal period ending before a decision date does not mean the report was public by then, a report is admitted only if it was available strictly before that date. Availability is taken as the later of two dates: the fiscal period end plus the statutory filing deadline, and any filing date supplied by the source. p8   Insert             In a representative screening run, the relevance filter retained 110 of 1,382 collected articles (8.0%) under strict investment-relevance criteria.
p8   Insert             To check the screen against human judgment, we blind-labeled a stratified sample of 180 of these articles (70 screen-passed and 110 screen-rejected) without access to the screen's decisions. The screen achieved a precision of 100% (95% Wilson confidence interval 94.8–100%): every article it passed was independently judged relevant, so the downstream stages are not contaminated by off-topic news. It is, however, deliberately conservative — re-weighting to the 8.0% population pass rate gives an estimated recall of 16% and a false-omission rate of 45% — indicating a high relevance bar rather than exhaustive retrieval; because the pipeline requires a focused, high-precision input set rather than complete coverage, precision is the more consequential property for downstream quality, and we note the lower recall as a limitation. Human–screen agreement was positive but modest (Cohen's kappa = 0.17), a value held down by the low base rate of passed articles, for which kappa is known to understate agreement. The screen is applied by the model without human adjudication during normal operation.

p24  Insert             Benchmark Robustness, Ablations, and Weighting The S&P 500 comparison overstates selection skill, because the eligible universe is concentrated in technology and semiconductor names that outran the broad market over the study period. Against progressively harder benchmarks, GPT-5.1’s pooled beat rate falls from 96.5% (S&P 500) to 94.3% (S&P 500 Information Technology, XLK), then to 39.6% against an equal-weighted portfolio of the eligible universe itself and 32.2% against a pure-semiconductor index (SMH), the hardest comparison because semiconductors led the sector over the study period. The filtering effect, however, survives these harder comparisons: 20→10 outperforms the equal-weighted universe in 81.4% of runs and SMH in 68.3%, and 10→5 in 64.8% and 60.0% respectively, while the unfiltered baseline does so in only 18.5% and 6.7%. All comparisons in this subsection are pooled across the full run set. The eligible comparison set is a fixed list of 29 large-capitalization technology and semiconductor companies (Supplementary Information), used only for the unfiltered baseline and as the draw pool for the equal-weighted and random benchmarks; the filtered configurations never see it, nominating candidates freely from screened news. Every constituent was continuously listed across the full evaluation span, so none was acquired or delisted and the benchmark needs no point-in-time reconstruction. The residual exposure is omission: companies large at the earliest window starts but no longer qualifying would not appear. The set does retain constituents whose prices declined materially, so it is not a selection of winners only, but this bounds the benchmark rather than the pipeline. Random portfolios drawn from the eligible universe, matched to each portfolio’s holding count and equal-weighted, provide a direct test of selection skill: relative to 4,000 such draws per run, the median GPT-5.1 portfolio under 20→10 sits at the 75th percentile and under 10→5 at the 69th, whereas the unfiltered baseline sits at the 40th percentile and the aggressive 30-pool configurations near the 17th–21st. Moderate two-stage filtering is thus the difference between below-random and above-random selection within the sector. Two ablations probe the robustness of the effect. Removing the single largest return contributor from every portfolio lowers GPT-5.1’s 20→10 mean ROI from 60.6% to 52.5% and the unfiltered baseline from 45.7% to 39.4%, so the matched filtering edge narrows only modestly, from about +14.9 to +13.1 percentage points; excluding all semiconductor holdings (with weights renormalized) leaves a smaller edge of +3.0 points. The filtering advantage therefore does not rest on any single position—it survives removing each portfolio’s best contributor largely intact—but it operates substantially through semiconductor selection: once the semiconductor sector as a whole is excluded, the matched edge, while still positive, is modest. Finally, re-scoring every portfolio at equal weights isolates stock selection from allocation weighting: GPT-5.1’s model-assigned weights cost 4.6 percentage points of mean ROI relative to equal-weighting the same picks (Wilcoxon p < 0.0001; GPT-4o −1.2, GPT-4o-mini ≈ 0). The models’ selection adds value; their position sizing subtracts from it. Sector-index returns, random-portfolio percentiles, ablation tables, and per-configuration risk metrics (annualized volatility, maximum drawdown, Sharpe, Sortino) are provided in the Supplementary Information. Risk-adjusted performance moves in the same direction as raw return. For GPT-5.1, the best configuration (20→10) earns a median Sharpe ratio of 1.99 and Sortino of 2.69, against 1.49 and 2.10 for the unfiltered baseline and roughly 0.83 for the S&P 500 over the same windows; the weakest configuration (30→5) falls to 1.35. Annualized volatility is similar across configurations (about 23–28%) and maximum drawdowns cluster near −22%, so the return gains from moderate filtering are not obtained by taking proportionally more risk. Full volatility, drawdown, and risk-adjusted statistics for every configuration and model are reported in the Supplementary Information.

p25  ParagraphProperty  Several limitations bound the scope of these findings. All portfolios are long-only and buy-and-hold, and returns are computed without transaction costs, taxes, or slippage, which would modestly reduce realized performance. The investment universe is restrictedconfined to a fixed set ofthe large-capitalization technology and semiconductor equitiessector, so the sector-contribution results in particular may not generalize to broader markets. Because GPT-5.1's evaluation windows are constrained to begin after its training cutoff, its results rest on fewer rolling windows (October 2024 onward) than the older models, and all windows fall within a broadly rising market; performance in a sustained downturn is therefore untested. Finally, the analysis reports raw and benchmark-relative returns together with the coefficient of variation, but does not compute risk-adjusted measures such as the Sharpe or Sortino ratio or maximum drawdown, because the per-portfolio daily return series required for them were not retainedBecause the best configuration (20→10) was identified as the maximum mean ROI across ten configurations evaluated on the same windows used to report it, the headline 60.6% carries a degree of selection inflation; the matched-baseline win rate and its exact confidence interval, which do not depend on selecting the top configuration after the fact, are the more conservative summary. p25  ParagraphProperty  Conclusion
p28  SectionProperty    Conclusion This study set out to test whether structuring the information given to a language model could improve long-term stock selection. Across 2,759 portfolio runs, a two-stage pipeline — using news to assemble a candidate shortlist and financial reports to validate it — reliably outperformed both unfiltered single-pass prompting and aggressive over-filtering. The largest gains came from a moderate design: starting from about ten to twenty candidates and narrowing them by roughly half (the 2× rule)— a ratio we present as a working hypothesis consistent with the tested grid — added on the order of fifteen percentage points of mean ROI over the unfiltered baseline and beat it in most matched evaluation windows. The central takeaway is that information design is a distinct and inexpensive determinant of performance in LLM-based investing, comparable in importance to the choice of model. Subject to the limitations above, this points toward a broader direction for LLM agents: treating the structure of a model's inputs as a first-class design variable rather than an afterthought.
